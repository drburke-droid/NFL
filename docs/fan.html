Why this exists, and how accuracy is measured — tap to read
Why this exists. Behind this page is Burke_v1, an NFL projection model that already ranks with the best fantasy sites in the business — CBS, ESPN, FantasyPros, FantasySharks, FFToday, numberFire, FleaFlicker and NFL.com — and goes head-to-head with the DraftKings player-prop lines every game. The question this page exists to answer is whether people and the machine together can build a better model than either alone: one sharp fan, or the combined lean of many, might see things the numbers don't. Every arrow you place is scored against what really happened, and the calls that keep landing closer than Burke_v1's earn a place in it.
How accuracy is measured, in plain words. Every projection is a guess at a player's fantasy points. After the game we take the difference between the guess and what he really scored, ignore whether it was too high or too low, and average those misses over every skill player Burke_v1 projected that week — players ruled out included, at zero, so catching an inactive in time counts as a hit and missing one counts against. A 4.0 means the typical projection was about four fantasy points off. Lower is better. Points are DraftKings scoring (full PPR, 4-point passing TD, −1 per interception and fumble lost, +3 at 300 passing, 100 rushing or 100 receiving yards), the scoring the big sites publish their own accuracy in. The big sites' figures are the ones they publish.

Man Meets Machine in Search of the Perfect Projection
Burke_v1 already ranks with the best fantasy sites in the business. It is not looking for a rival. It is looking for a partner.
From the desk of the model · Kuhn & Friends, and anyone else who wants in

Every projection system is blind to something. A model can read ten seasons of box scores, the weather, the Vegas line and the injury report, and still not know that a receiver limped off the practice field on Friday, that a coach has fallen out of love with his running back, or that a rookie has the whole locker room believing. People know those things. They just have never had a way to prove it.

This page is that way. Each week Burke_v1 posts its projected stat line for every skill player on the slate. You read them and, where you know better, you say so: a tap of ▲ adds a set amount — half a touchdown, a catch, ten yards — and a tap of ▼ takes it away. Leave alone whatever you agree with. Press Lock them in and your arrows become a model of their own — you + Burke_v1 — that is scored after the games exactly the way the machine is scored: on every player it projected, by how many fantasy points it was off.

The homer's edge

There is reason to think the fans will hold their own. The league behind this page has run for twenty-two seasons, and its draft history keeps whispering the same thing: the dart throws land a little more often when the thrower knows the team. A dart throw, in this league's language, is a cheap late-auction buy — a dollar or two, three at most — on a player the consensus rankings do not rate, taken on a hunch; it counts as a hit if he ends up worth a starting spot. Most darts miss, which is what makes them darts. But the ones thrown at a manager's own team, the roster he watches every week and argues about at dinner, have hit at a slightly better clip than the ones thrown at everyone else's. That is a small edge in a small sample, and the market prices those players the same for everybody. It is exactly the kind of thing this page is built to test properly: does knowing a team well tell you something the numbers miss, and can a model be taught to listen?

A contest, and a collaboration

The standings above make it a contest, and it should be one. But the prize is not a trophy. Every arrow is an experiment. If your calls keep landing closer than the machine's on a kind of player it misjudges — tight ends, say, or the backs on your own team — that is a signal, and signals earn weight. The version of Burke_v1 that ships next season will carry the fingerprints of whoever turned out to be right, and the email box on the game is how we find you when it is time to build it.

There is a stranger prize, too. A fan who is reliably wrong is as useful as one who is reliably right — maybe more so, because a consistent miss is a signal the model can simply flip. If you fade a player every time the crowd is high on him and he keeps going off anyway, the model learns to do the opposite of you, and it gets better for it. So getting things right is not the only way to leave a mark. Getting things wrong the same way, week after week, leaves one too. The only fan who teaches the machine nothing is the one who is right about half the time.

Coming to these pages: The Crowd + Burke_v1, a single model built from everyone's arrows at once, so we can see whether many partial views add up to a better one than any of us holds alone.

The fine print

Burke_v1 and every fan model are graded on the same player-games: every skill player projected that week, a player ruled out counting as zero. Arrows lock at kickoff. A win in the standings is a week with a lower error than Burke_v1's; a fan who places no arrows in a week is neither scored nor charged for it. Your username and PIN are you, on any device. And the big sites are listed with the accuracy they publish themselves, so nobody is grading them but them.
