Man Meets Machine
THE ORACLE already ranks with the best projection sites in the business. It is not looking for a rival. It is looking for a partner.
From the desk of the model

Every projection system is blind to something. A model can read ten seasons of box scores, the Vegas line and the injury report, and still not know that a receiver limped off the practice field on Friday, or that a coach has fallen out of love with his running back. Fans know those things. They have just never had a way to prove it.

This is the way. Each week THE ORACLE posts its projection for every skill player on the slate. Boost the ones you think it has too low, fade the ones you think it has too high, and lock them in. Your picks become a model of their own — you + THE ORACLE — scored after the games exactly the way the machine is scored.

The prize

Not a trophy. Every pick is an experiment. If your calls keep landing closer than the machine's on a kind of player it misjudges, that is a signal, and signals earn weight: next season's ORACLE will carry the fingerprints of whoever turned out to be right. Being reliably wrong counts too — a consistent miss is a signal the model can simply flip. The only fan who teaches it nothing is the one who never plays.

The fine print

Every model is graded on the same player-games: every skill player projected that week, a player ruled out counting as zero. Picks lock at kickoff. A win is a week with a lower error than THE ORACLE's; a week with no picks is neither scored nor charged. The big sites are listed with the accuracy they publish themselves.

What this is, and why from the model's owner

Why. THE ORACLE is an NFL projection model that ranks with CBS, ESPN, FantasyPros, FantasySharks, FFToday, numberFire, FleaFlicker and NFL.com, and goes head-to-head with the sportsbook prop lines every game. The question this page asks is simple: can people and the machine together beat either one alone?

How accuracy is measured. A projection is a guess at a player's fantasy points. After the game we take the gap between the guess and the box score, ignore which way it missed, and average it over every skill player projected that week, inactives at zero. A 4.0 means the typical projection was four points off. Lower is better. Points are full-PPR fantasy scoring, the same scoring the big sites publish their accuracy in.
